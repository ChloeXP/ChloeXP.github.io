01

The problem

Transilien SNCF Voyageurs runs the suburban rail network of Île-de-France: more than 6,200 trains and about 3.4 million passengers a day. Platform screens announce how many minutes remain until the next train. The goal of the challenge is to make that estimate more accurate.

What we predict: p0q0

p0q0 = theoretical waiting time − actual waiting time, as an integer number of minutes. A negative value means passengers waited longer than announced (the train was late); a positive value means they waited less; zero means the screen was right.

Example. The screen shows 3 minutes, the passenger actually waits 5 minutes → p0q0 = 3 − 5 = −2.

Inputs

VariableMeaningType
trainTrain number (unique per day)Context
gareStation ID (anonymised)Context
dateDateContext
arretPosition of the stop along the train’s routeContext
p0q2 / p0q3 / p0q4Deviation of the same train 2, 3 and 4 stations earlierHistory
p2q0 / p3q0 / p4q0Deviation of the 2nd, 3rd and 4th previous trains at the same stationHistory

Real-time constraint: there is no p0q1 or p1q0. The prediction must be made two stations ahead, when the most recent stop has not happened yet.

Why the metric matters: MAE averages absolute errors without squaring them, so it is far less sensitive to extreme values than MSE. Under MAE the best constant prediction is the median, not the mean — a fact that shaped most of the decisions below.

03

Data exploration

The aim was not to draw many plots, but to answer the questions that change later decisions.

Target distribution

The median of p0q0 is 0 and 42.7% of values are exactly 0. The mean is −0.16, but the range is very asymmetric: from −160 (severe delays) to +15. Concentrated around zero with a long negative tail — a distribution better described by its median.

All test dates come after all training dates. This single fact determined the validation scheme.

Which categories are “known” at test time?

99.98% of test train numbers never appear in training — train IDs are re-issued daily, so train cannot be a feature. All 84 stations, on the other hand, are present in both sets, so gare is safe to use.

A rejected hypothesis

The first trains of the day cannot have “previous trains”, yet the data has no missing values. I suspected missing values were filled with 0. But 0 appears in 43.8% of p2q0 versus 42.7% of the target — no sign of mass zero-filling, so the hypothesis was dropped.

04

Validation scheme

The test set has no labels and submissions are limited, so I needed a local “mock exam”. A random split would be wrong: rows from the same day share the same disruptions (a morning incident delays almost every train). Splitting a day across train and validation lets the model see that day’s chaos during training and inflates the score — data leakage.

Instead, I split by whole days in time order: x and y are first joined so they move together, sorted by date with a stable sort, and cut at the day containing the 80th percentile row.

PartDatesRows
Training2023-04-03 → 2023-10-02529,328
Validation2023-10-03 → 2023-11-10 (≈ 5.5 weeks)137,936

The validation set reproduces the test set’s structure: 100% of its train numbers are unseen (99.98% in the test set). Across later submissions, local and leaderboard scores moved in the same direction — with one instructive exception (section 06).

PITFALL AVOIDED

Sorting x without y would silently pair every row with someone else’s answer — no error, just a wrong model. Joining y to x before sorting prevents it.

05

Modelling

Baseline: random forest — and why rounding helps

Features: arret, the six p/q columns, a label-encoded station (one shared, sorted dictionary over train ∪ test so each station always gets the same code) and the day of week. train and date were excluded: unseen IDs, and future dates a tree cannot extrapolate to.

MethodValidation MAE
Predict 0 everywhere0.873
Random forest (100 trees, depth 15)0.764
Random forest + rounding0.712

Rounding cut MAE by 7%. A random forest minimises squared error and outputs means (e.g. 0.25), but the target is an integer concentrated at 0, so its median is usually an integer. Rounding pushes predictions toward that median. It is a patch for a loss/metric mismatch; the real fix came in step 5. First submission (v0): leaderboard 0.747, rank 479.

Feature engineering

Aggregates and trends: 0.712 → 0.693. Station target statistics: → 0.682 → 0.681, with diminishing returns since the station ID was already a feature. Target statistics are computed on the training part only — including validation labels would leak the answer.

LightGBM with an L1 objective

Twice the data had said the competition rewards predicting the median. Gradient boosting with objective = "l1" optimises MAE directly; early stopping (100 rounds of patience) picks the number of trees. Changing only the loss — 0.681 → 0.661 — beat all feature engineering combined. For the full-data submission I used a fixed number of rounds scaled to the extra data and checked that test columns matched training columns in name and order. Submission v1: leaderboard 0.680, rank 288.

Tuning tree size

Halving the learning rate at 511 leaves gained only 0.0003 for almost twice the training time, so 0.1 was kept. Submission v3: leaderboard 0.659, rank 121.

Seed ensemble

With feature_fraction and bagging_fraction at 0.8, models trained with different seeds make different errors that partly cancel out. Without randomness, changing the seed gives almost the same model and averaging is useless.

Submission v4: leaderboard 0.6532, rank 72 / 665.

06

Two failed experiments

Both were removed, and both taught more than most successes.

Station × stop target encoding — leakage through small groups

Hypothesis: the same station behaves differently by line and direction, which arret partly captures. I added the mean target per (station, stop) pair.

VersionStopping roundMAEMAE rounded
Without the feature10540.67350.661
With it, no smoothing5170.67780.672
With it, smoothing m = 504690.67580.669

Diagnosis: each row’s own answer is part of its group mean. 772 of the 1,477 pairs have fewer than 30 rows; with one row, the “feature” is the label. Smoothing — (n × group mean + m × global mean) / (n + m) — confirmed the diagnosis (0.672 → 0.669) but still lost to having no feature. LightGBM already sees both columns and learns their interaction itself.

Daily average — better locally, worse on the leaderboard

Hypothesis: some days the whole network struggles. I added the same-day mean of p0q2 (features only, no labels).

07

A better ruler: multi-window validation

After v4, remaining improvements were in the 0.001–0.003 range, and a single validation split could no longer separate them from luck: the learning-rate change differed by 0.0003, and the daily-mean feature had already fooled the local score once. Before any new feature, I needed a more precise measuring tool.

Design choices

  • Expanding, not sliding windows. The final model is trained on all data from April 3rd, so every validation fold trains from April 3rd as well. Whether old data is stale is a separate question, tested later as its own experiment.
  • 3 windows × 15 days. A validation window does not need to be as long as the test set: 15 days already hold about 110,000 rows. Shorter windows leave 46 days of training for the first fold, about half the data.
  • Label-based features recomputed per window. Station target statistics use only that window’s training part; row-level features are shared.

Harder exam, or weaker model?

Measuring the ruler’s own error

Changing only the seed (1 → 2) moved the 3-window average by 0.0006, and window 3 alone by 0.001. That is pure noise, so it defines the bar. From here on, an experiment is kept only if:

  1. the 3-window average MAE improves by at least 0.001;
  2. no window gets worse;
  3. window 3, closest to the test period, gets particular attention.

The whole pipeline — split, station statistics, training, scoring, out-of-fold predictions — was wrapped in one function so each experiment is a single call. Saving out-of-fold predictions let me test rounding offsets and blends in seconds, without retraining.

What the experiments showed

  • The ruler stopped a false improvement. The rounding offset looked slightly better on average, but later windows preferred c = 0 and the test period is later still.
  • Out-of-fold encoding fixes leakage, not information. The station × stop feature went from −0.011 to roughly neutral, but added nothing a 1,023-leaf tree could not already learn.
  • Beware of after-the-fact stories. I first explained the time-decay result by “summer holiday data” — the training set has no July or August data at all. A second tempting pattern (p0q2 equals the target two stops earlier) matched at exactly the rate of chance (~27%) and could not even be paired in the test set.
  • Window 3 barely moves. With 555,000 training rows, structure tuning helps mainly the earlier, data-poorer windows.

10

Lessons learned

  1. Align the objective with the metric. Rounding (0.764 → 0.712) and the L1 loss (0.681 → 0.661) were the two largest jumps — bigger than any feature.
  2. Make validation look like the test. Test dates are all in the future, so split by time and by whole days.
  3. Build the ruler before running experiments. When gains are around 0.001, measure noise first and judge with several windows.
  4. Consistency beats the average. A change that helps on average but hurts one window is not reliable.
  5. Small groups leak labels. Check group sizes before target encoding; smooth or use out-of-fold encoding.
  6. The leaderboard is the final judge. Drop what fails there, and record why.
  7. Test hypotheses, don’t tell stories. Two plausible explanations were refuted by a few seconds of data checks.
  8. Know when to stop. Five of eight experiments did nothing: the data’s information was largely used up.

Limits and next steps

  • Only six historical deviations, the station and the stop position are available; recent leaderboard gains are close to leaderboard noise (about 20,000 test rows).
  • Why the daily-mean feature failed on the leaderboard is still unexplained.
  • A final run with learning rate 0.05 and 20 seeds might add 0.0003–0.0008.
